I study how metal nanoparticles confine light and sound. Silver and gold particles, from a few to a few hundred nanometres across, concentrate optical fields through surface plasmons and vibrate at gigahertz to terahertz frequencies.

I make the particles, measure them, and model them with finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations. This site lets you play with simplified versions of those models and use the calculators I use at the bench.

Confined phonons

A nanoparticle is a tiny elastic resonator. Its acoustic vibrations are quantised by its size, so smaller particles ring faster. The simplest one, the breathing mode, makes the whole sphere expand and contract.

For a free homogeneous sphere of radius R, the breathing-mode frequency is f = ξcL / (2πR), where cL is the longitudinal sound velocity and ξ is the first root of the Lamb equation tan ξ = 4ξ / (4 − (cL/cT)² ξ²). Pump–probe experiments detect this mode as an oscillation of the optical transmission.

Model and limits

This is the textbook model of a free, isotropic sphere in vacuum, with bulk sound velocities. Real particles are damped by their surroundings (solvent, substrate, ligands), are not perfectly spherical, and gold and silver are elastically anisotropic, which shifts and splits modes. Higher-order modes (quadrupolar, l = 2) are not shown here.

Confined surface plasmons

In a metal nanoparticle, the conduction electrons oscillate collectively with the light field. The resulting localized surface plasmon resonance sets the colour of a colloid and concentrates light at the particle surface.

Silver and gold NPs synthesis

Colloidal gold and silver particles are made by reducing a metal salt in the presence of a stabiliser. The reducing agent, the stabiliser and the temperature decide the size, the shape and therefore the colour.

Reference recipes

Gold nanospheres, citrate reduction (Turkevich–Frens)about 12–20 nm, peak near 520 nm

Reagents

  • HAuCl4, 1 mM in water (20 mL)
  • Trisodium citrate, 1 wt% (about 2 mL, 38.8 mM)

Tuning

Less citrate gives larger particles, but a broader size distribution.

Procedure

  1. Bring the HAuCl4 solution to a rolling boil under stirring, in clean glassware.
  2. Add the citrate quickly. The colour goes from pale yellow to grey-blue, then to wine red.
  3. Keep boiling for about 15 minutes, then cool to room temperature under stirring.

Check

UV–vis peak at 518–525 nm. A peak above 530 nm or a purple tint points to aggregation or larger particles.

Silver nanospheres, citrate reduction (Lee–Meisel)about 50–100 nm, polydisperse, peak near 420 nm

Reagents

  • AgNO3, 90 mg in 500 mL water
  • Trisodium citrate, 1 wt% (10 mL)

Tuning

Strongly polydisperse. For smaller, more uniform particles use a NaBH4 seed route.

Procedure

  1. Bring the silver nitrate solution to a boil under stirring.
  2. Add the citrate solution and keep boiling for about one hour.
  3. The suspension turns greenish-grey. Cool under stirring and store in the dark.

Check

A broad band around 400–450 nm, often with a shoulder. Silver oxidises: measure soon after synthesis.

Gold nanorods, seed-mediated growth (CTAB)aspect ratio 2–5, longitudinal peak 650–900 nm

Reagents

  • HAuCl4, CTAB, ice-cold NaBH4 for the seeds
  • Growth solution: HAuCl4, CTAB, AgNO3, ascorbic acid

Tuning

The amount of Ag+ in the growth solution mainly sets the aspect ratio.

Procedure

  1. Make small seeds by reducing HAuCl4 in CTAB with NaBH4. Use them after about 30 minutes.
  2. Prepare the growth solution and add ascorbic acid until it turns colourless.
  3. Add a small volume of seeds, mix briefly, then leave undisturbed overnight at about 30 °C.

Check

Two bands: transverse near 520 nm and longitudinal at 650–900 nm. Purify by centrifugation to remove excess CTAB.

Silver nanocubes, polyol synthesis (PVP)edge 40–80 nm, peak 430–490 nm

Reagents

  • Ethylene glycol (solvent and reducing agent)
  • AgNO3, PVP, a trace of chloride or sulphide

Tuning

Cube size depends on reaction time and on the amount of silver precursor.

Procedure

  1. Heat ethylene glycol to about 150 °C in an oil bath.
  2. Add the trace additive, then PVP and AgNO3 solutions in ethylene glycol.
  3. Follow the colour and the UV–vis spectrum, and stop the reaction by cooling when the target band appears.

Check

Sharp peak with a clear shoulder at shorter wavelengths. Confirm shape and edge length by electron microscopy.

Flocculation calculator

A charged colloid stays dispersed as long as electrostatic repulsion keeps particles apart. Salt screens the charge, the barrier collapses and the particles aggregate. This calculator uses DLVO theory for two identical spheres in water at 25 °C.

Interaction energy between two particles in units of kBT against the gap between their surfaces. A positive hump is the barrier that keeps the colloid stable.

Stability ratio against salt concentration (log–log, capped at 10¹²). The dashed line marks the estimated critical coagulation concentration.

Assumptions

  • Two identical spheres, constant surface potential, Derjaguin approximation for the double-layer repulsion, and the exact van der Waals expression for two spheres (no retardation).
  • No steric, hydration or specific-adsorption forces. Ligand-coated particles can be much more stable than DLVO predicts.
  • The surface potential does not change with salt. In reality the potential falls as counter-ions adsorb, especially for multivalent salts.
  • The stability ratio W is the Fuchs integral, with the gap cut off at 0.3 nm. The half-time is the Smoluchowski fast-aggregation time 3η / (4 kBT n0) multiplied by W, with η = 0.89 mPa·s.
  • The critical coagulation concentration is taken where W falls below 10, an operational definition.

Defaults are illustrative. Adjust the surface potential and Hamaker constant to your system, for example from a zeta-potential measurement.
